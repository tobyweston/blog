You know the phrase. You might have said it yourself, or had it said to you. It usually arrives in a reply-all email or a pointed Slack message after someone has pushed back on something:

> _"I wish you would put as much energy into fixing the issue as you do arguing against it."_

It sounds reasonable. But it falls apart quickly, and the damage is real.

## What it's really saying

On the surface the message is: _get on with it_. But the subtext is more corrosive:

- Your concern is not welcome here
- Raising an objection is the opposite of being useful
- Passion about a topic is a liability, not an asset

The person on the receiving end doesn't just hear "let's move forward." They hear "you talk too much." And next time, they will.

## The fallacy

The phrase implies that arguing and fixing compete for the same energy. As though the two are drawing from the same finite pool. As though passion about a topic is energy subtracted from action on it.

Neither of those things is true. The amount of energy someone invests in raising a concern tells you nothing about how much energy they will invest in resolving it. The two run on entirely separate tracks.

This is a **false dilemma** — the assumption that arguing and fixing are mutually exclusive choices. It's also a **non sequitur**: there's no logical connection between the two. People who argue strongly about something typically care deeply enough to fix it. The passion is the signal. Dismissing it doesn't redirect energy toward the solution — it removes the most motivated person from the conversation.

## It sidesteps the substance

The phrase doesn't engage with the argument being made. It doesn't say _you're wrong because X_. It says _the fact that you're making an argument at all is the problem_. This is an **ad hominem** move — the target shifts from the idea to the behaviour of the person raising it.

The underlying concern doesn't disappear when the conversation is shut down. It just goes underground.

Rhetological fallacies tend to work by changing the subject. This one changes the subject from "is the concern valid?" to "why are you being difficult?" And it usually wins by exhaustion, not by reason.

## What it does to psychological safety

Amy Edmondson's research on psychological safety is clear on this: teams perform better when people feel safe to speak up, challenge assumptions, and raise concerns without fear of punishment or ridicule. That safety isn't a nice-to-have — it's the mechanism by which teams catch errors, surface problems early, and avoid groupthink.

The phrase frames challenge as unproductive. It positions the person raising concerns as an obstacle. It attaches a social cost to dissent.

You may not mean it that way. But you've just told everyone in earshot that speaking up has consequences. The most senior person in the thread might weather it — they have the standing. The more junior person reading along will make a very different calculation.

## You can hold both

Arguing against something and working to fix it are not mutually exclusive. I can believe the current approach is wrong _and_ be working to resolve the underlying problem. I can push back on a decision _and_ implement it if it goes ahead. These happen on different tracks. One doesn't consume the other.

Getting the right people to name the problem clearly, challenge the framing, and offer alternatives is often where the real work happens — before a line of code is written or a decision is made. Shutting that down in the name of "just getting on with it" doesn't save time. It borrows against it.

## What to say instead

If someone is raising the same concern repeatedly and it's blocking progress, the problem isn't that they're arguing. The problem is that they haven't been heard, or the decision-making process isn't clear.

Try instead:

- _"I hear your concern. Here's why we're making this call anyway, and here's when we'll revisit it."_
- _"Let's timebox it. We'll try this approach for two sprints and review."_
- _"Can you write up the specific risks you're worried about? I want to make sure we've genuinely considered them."_

None of those require the person to stop caring.

## The version to retire

The next time you feel the phrase forming — "put as much energy into fixing it as arguing against it" — stop and ask what you're actually trying to say. If it's _I need a decision made and we're going in circles_, say that. If it's _I'm not convinced this concern is being raised in good faith_, say that too, and have that conversation directly.

Don't use a logical fallacy to shut someone down and call it pragmatism. The argument doesn't go away. The person just stops making it out loud.
