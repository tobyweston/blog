You know the phrase. You might have said it yourself, or had it said to you. It usually arrives in a reply-all email or a pointed Slack message after someone has pushed back on something:

> _"I wish you would put as much energy into fixing the issue as you do arguing against it."_

It feels like a reasonable thing to say. Pragmatic, even. But unpick it slightly and it falls apart quickly — and the damage it does is real.

## What it's really saying

On the surface, the message is: _get on with it_. But the subtext is more corrosive:

- Your concern is not welcome here
- Raising an objection is the opposite of being useful
- Passion about a topic is a liability, not an asset

The person on the receiving end doesn't just hear "let's move forward." They hear "you talk too much." And next time, they will.

## The fallacy

There's a specific error in logic here, and it's worth naming it.

The phrase implies that the energy spent arguing comes at the direct expense of fixing. As though the two activities are drawing from the same finite pool. As though passion about a topic is energy subtracted from action on it.

This is a **false dilemma** — the assumption that we must choose one or the other. It's also a **non sequitur**: the amount of energy someone invests in raising a concern tells you nothing about how much energy they will invest in resolving it. The two are not in competition. They are often entirely unrelated.

In fact, the inverse is usually true. People who argue strongly about something typically care deeply enough _to fix it_. The passion is the signal. Dismissing it doesn't redirect energy toward the solution — it removes the most motivated person from the conversation.

## It sidesteps the substance entirely

There's a second problem, and it's arguably worse.

The phrase doesn't engage with the argument being made. It doesn't say _you're wrong because X_. It says _the fact that you're making an argument at all is the problem_. This is an **ad hominem** move — shifting the target from the idea to the behaviour of the person raising it.

The practical effect: the underlying issue, the one being argued about, goes unaddressed. The concern doesn't disappear when the conversation is shut down. It just goes underground.

Rhetological fallacies tend to work by changing the subject. This one changes the subject from "is the concern valid?" to "why are you being difficult?" And it usually wins by exhaustion, not by reason.

## What it does to psychological safety

Amy Edmondson's research on psychological safety is clear on this: teams perform better when people feel safe to speak up, challenge assumptions, and raise concerns without fear of punishment or ridicule. That safety isn't a nice-to-have. It's the mechanism by which teams catch errors, surface problems early, and avoid groupthink.

The phrase does the opposite. It frames challenge as unproductive. It positions the person raising concerns as an obstacle. It attaches a social cost to dissent.

You may not mean it that way. But you've just told everyone in earshot that speaking up has consequences. The most senior person in the thread might not be silenced — they have the standing to weather it. The more junior person reading along will make a very different calculation.

## You can hold both

Here's the thing the phrase misunderstands most fundamentally: arguing against something and working to fix it are not mutually exclusive. I can believe the current approach is wrong _and_ be working to resolve the underlying problem. I can push back on a decision _and_ implement it if it goes ahead. These happen on different tracks. One doesn't consume the other.

In a functional team, robust discussion _is_ part of fixing things. Getting the right people to name the problem clearly, challenge the framing, and offer alternatives is often where the real work happens — before a line of code is written or a decision is made. Shutting that down in the name of "just getting on with it" doesn't save time. It borrows against it.

## What to say instead

If someone is raising the same concern repeatedly and it's blocking progress, the problem isn't that they're arguing. The problem is that they haven't been heard, or the decision-making process isn't clear.

Try instead:

- _"I hear your concern. Here's why we're making this call anyway, and here's when we'll revisit it."_
- _"Let's agree to timeboxed. We'll try this approach for two sprints and review."_
- _"Can you write up the specific risks you're worried about? I want to make sure we've genuinely considered them."_

These responses take the concern seriously and create a path forward. They don't ask someone to stop caring.

## The version to retire

The next time you feel the phrase forming — "put as much energy into fixing it as arguing against it" — stop and ask what you're actually trying to say. If it's _I need a decision made and we're going in circles_, say that. If it's _I'm not convinced this concern is being raised in good faith_, say that too, and have that conversation directly.

But don't use a logical fallacy to shut someone down and call it pragmatism. The argument doesn't go away. The person just stops making it out loud.
