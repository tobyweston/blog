Continuing from the previous post on [attestations and evidence](/blog/2026-10-02-attestations-and-evidence/), this post looks deeper into defining policy as code, evaluating and producing a consistent evidence format that shows _why_ a control passed or failed.

## Problems with Traditional Evidence

If you were to pick a release at random in a large regulated organisation and look at how controls were closed, you might find something like this.

| Control                           | FINOS Migitation                                                                                            | Format | Closure   | Description                                                  |
|-----------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------|-----------|:-------------------------------------------------------------|
| Never alone applied               | [Code Review](https://sdlc-common-controls.finos.org/mitigations/mi-1_code-review.html)                     | JSON   | automated | Evidence of a PR being merged and branch protection in place |
| Sufficient testing was undertaken | [Test Execution and Sign-Off](https://sdlc-common-controls.finos.org/mitigations/mi-16_test-execution.html) | PDF    | automated | JUnit reports demonstrating some tests were executed         |
| SBOM available                    | [Component Inventory](https://sdlc-common-controls.finos.org/mitigations/mi-9-component-inventory.html)     | .xlsx  | manual    | Provenance unknown - we don't know who generated this or how |

None of these are obviously provable to apply to the same artifact. How do I know the tests that were run are relevant to the artefact we're deploying?

Each one has a different format and might be highly technical in nature. A typical PR log will have Git SHAs and branch names, something a non-technical auditor might not recognise. It's not obvious that the list of SHAs relates to the artifact being deployed. Perhaps worse, none of these describe the rules by which compliance can be determined. The list of PRs doesn't include a description that "all code has been through a PR" or that "no author can be the reviewer". That second part is hidden in the runtime policy enforcmcent of branch protection... or not. The point is, no one can really tell by just looking at the evidence. We need more.

None of them says what was checked, against which version of which rule, using what inputs. The JSON is a payload from whichever tool happened to close the control. The PDF is a screenshot of a dashboard that has since been re-rendered. The spreadsheet is a human asserting a conclusion.

The problem isn't the volume of evidence. It's that every control invented its own shape, so the auditor has to learn all of them and still ends up taking the result on trust. A ticket stub, not CCTV.

## Modern Evidence

When evidence just shows the output of a process, an auditor has no choice but to look into that process. When its tooling, they can inspect the configuration (for example, look at your branch protection rules) but at scale that can become time consuming and unreliable, especially when you need to demonstrate it six months ago. For manual processes, it quickly becomes impracticable.

To be able to independently verify and re-check a decision, and to be able to do that three years in the future, your evidence will need:

* an identifiable artifact (a fingerprint of the subject of the claim)
* the facts that were used, and where each one came from
* the rules that were applied, verbatim and versioned
* how the fact met the rules (or otherwise)
* the result, and when it was reached

Read that list again and notice what isn't in it. Nothing specific to a control. Nothing about code review, test coverage or CVEs.

> It's because there are **no control-specific items** in the list, that we can create a single evidence format that will satisfy an auditor for **any control**.

## The Baking Example

The bakery example is useful as the production line is very similar to the software supply chain. Imagine the chef who is baking a batch of cakes. She wants to demonstrate the batch is allergen free so she can sell to the local millennials.

She's using the approach from the [previous post](/blog/2026-10-02-attestations-and-evidence/) and:

* recording the list of ingredients used, oven temperature and cooking times
* defining a policy to ensure no nuts are in the ingredients list
* expanding that policy to ensure baking temperatures and times are consistent to ingredients properly

She is capturing the policy as Rego and the ingredients and cooking details as attestations. The evaluation of the policy against the attestations produces a pass/fail decision in the format below.

## Attestations: What was observed

<ExampleCallout mascotAlt="Illustration of a chef giving a thumbs up">
The baker attests to the ingredients that went in, the temperature and time the oven ran at, the decorations added, and who signed the batch off. None of those statements know anything about allergen policy. They're just a record of what happened.

**`attestations.json`**

```json
      "value": 32, "source": "oven telemetry",
      "observed_at": "2026-05-07T10:16:10Z" }
  ]
}
```

## Policy: What should have been true

Within the evidence, the full policy is captured. Both as Rego (technically, the policy-as-code part) but also as a human-readable table of clauses. Rego can get pretty knarly and having a human readible summary is powerful for the non-technical audience. The fact that this is code and now hearsay or departmental memory, means trust levels go up when audit speaks to the teams operating the control.

> In plain language, an auditor can see the version of the policy that was actually used, elliminating the frantic scramble to demonstrate how a system would have behaved six months ago.


**`bakery.rego`**

```rego
package bakery

# Facts, projected from the attestation set. Each one remembers which attestation it came from.
fact[att.field] := {"value": att.value, "from": att.id} if {
	some att in input.attestations
}

default nut_free := false

nut_free if not "nuts" in fact.allergens.value

default temp_ok := false

temp_ok if {
	fact.temp_c.value >= 175
	fact.temp_c.value <= 200
}

default time_ok := false

time_ok if {
	fact.minutes.value >= 25
	fact.minutes.value <= 40
}

# The clause table: what each rule means, what it drew on, and what was actually evaluated.
clauses := [
	{"clause": "Nut allergens absent", "predicate": "nut_free", "holds": nut_free,
		"inputs": [fact.allergens.from],
		"expression": sprintf(`not "nuts" in %v`, [fact.allergens.value])},
	{"clause": "Bake temperature in range", "predicate": "temp_ok", "holds": temp_ok,
		"inputs": [fact.temp_c.from],
		"expression": sprintf("%v >= 175 and %v <= 200", [fact.temp_c.value, fact.temp_c.value])},
	{"clause": "Bake time in range", "predicate": "time_ok", "holds": time_ok,
		"inputs": [fact.minutes.from],
		"expression": sprintf("%v >= 25 and %v <= 40", [fact.minutes.value, fact.minutes.value])},
]
```

The `fact` partial object rule projects the set of attestations into something the clauses can read and helps each fact remembers the attestation it came from. That `from` field is what later lets the evidence cite a specific input value (`att-003` above) rather than just asserting that the temperature was 180. It will produce the following.

```json
{
  "ingredients": { "from": "att-001", "value": ["flour","eggs","milk","sugar","butter"] },
  "allergens":   { "from": "att-002", "value": ["milk","eggs"] },
  "temp_c":      { "from": "att-003", "value": 180 },
  "minutes":     { "from": "att-004", "value": 32 },
  "decorations": { "from": "att-005", "value": ["sprinkles"] },
  "signed_by":   { "from": "att-006", "value": "Baker A (emp-12345)" }
}
```

The `clauses` table is the policy describing itself. Each entry carries the human-readable statement, the attestations it drew on, and a rendered expression with the real values substituted in. It costs a few lines and it's the difference between a document that shows its workings and one that doesn't.

## Evaluation: Show your workings

The third section is the one almost no real evidence has, and the reason is usually that someone tried to write it by hand afterwards.

It shouldn't be written by hand. It should be output. A second, generic package turns any clause table into a decision, and this part is the same for every control in the estate:

**`evidence.rego`**

```rego
package evidence

import data.bakery

verdict(true) := "Pass"

verdict(false) := "Fail"

default compliant := false

compliant if every c in bakery.clauses {
	c.holds
}

status := "Compliant" if compliant

status := "Non-compliant" if not compliant

steps := [object.union(object.remove(c, ["holds"]), {
	"step": i + 1,
	"result": verdict(c.holds),
}) |
	some i, c in bakery.clauses
]
```

Run it:

```bash
opa eval --data bakery.rego --data evidence.rego \
  --input attestations.json 'data.evidence.decision'
```

```json
{
  "status": "Compliant",
  "steps": [
    { "step": 1, "clause": "Nut allergens absent", "predicate": "nut_free",
      "inputs": ["att-002"],
      "expression": "not \"nuts\" in [\"milk\", \"eggs\"]", "result": "Pass" },
    { "step": 2, "clause": "Bake temperature in range", "predicate": "temp_ok",
      "inputs": ["att-003"],
      "expression": "180 >= 175 and 180 <= 200", "result": "Pass" },
    { "step": 3, "clause": "Bake time in range", "predicate": "time_ok",
      "inputs": ["att-004"],
      "expression": "32 >= 25 and 32 <= 40", "result": "Pass" }
  ]
}
```

The rows in that third section of the document are that output, rendered. Not a reconstruction, not a narrative someone typed into a ticket afterwards. The workings are a by-product of the decision, which is the only way they can be trusted to match it.

Note where the split falls. `bakery.rego` is small, control-specific and reviewable — it's the thing that should go through governance. `evidence.rego` is infrastructure, written once. The conjunction ("compliant only if all clauses pass") lives in the infrastructure too, because it's the same sentence for every control.

## When it fails

Now run the same control over a batch where the oven drifted to 210°C.

<ClickToEnlarge src={evidenceFail} alt="An evidence document headed Allergen Free Cake Batch with a red shield and a NON-COMPLIANT badge, showing its evaluation section. Step two reads 210 >= 175 and 210 <= 200 with a Fail result; steps one and three pass, and the overall row reads Non-compliant."></ClickToEnlarge>


Same document, same sections, same code path. One expression now reads `210 >= 175 and 210 <= 200`, one verdict flips, and the overall status follows.

Three things fall out of that, and they're the whole argument for generating evidence rather than curating it.

Failure costs the same as success. There's no separate "exception process" that produces a different artefact, because there's no separate code path.

The reason is derived, not authored. Step 2 says why. Nobody writes a justification, which means nobody can write a convenient one.

And the attestation set hash changed, because the facts changed. The document is bound to the inputs that produced it.

## Why it has to be the same document every time

A uniform format isn't tidiness. It buys specific things.

**One format, N controls.** The auditor learns the document once. Onboarding cost is paid a single time rather than per control, and the comparison between controls becomes possible at all.

**The decision is falsifiable.** Canonical inputs, verbatim policy, both hashed. Anyone can re-run it and either get the same answer or not. That's a much stronger position than "trust the tool that closed the control", and it's the difference between evidence and assertion.

**The rule is on the page.** This one is underrated. When the policy is printed in the evidence, an auditor can challenge the *rule* rather than the outcome. Is 175–200°C actually the right range? That's a far more useful conversation than relitigating whether the control was closed, and today it's nearly impossible to have, because the rule is buried in a tool nobody outside the team can read.

**It works as a fitness function.** You cannot produce this document from a screenshot. You cannot produce it if the thing collecting the evidence also decides the outcome, because then there's nothing left to show as workings. Adopting the format forces controls towards declarative, opinionless attestations and external evaluation, which is where they should have been anyway. Mandating the output shape turns out to be a more effective lever than mandating the architecture.

**It's an interface.** The evidence schema becomes the contract between the teams implementing controls and the system of record that stores them. Contracts can be versioned and tested. "Upload something that demonstrates compliance" cannot.

And yes — the PDF is a rendering, not the record. The record is the structured decision; the document is the view of it for people, carrying the same hashes as the view for machines. Give the auditor the PDF and the pipeline the JSON, generated from one source, and they can never disagree.

## What it doesn't do

Some honesty about the limits, because a pretty document is a good way to smuggle in unearned confidence.

It doesn't make an attestation trustworthy. Signature verification, issuer identity and freshness all happen before the policy sees anything. The document records that they were checked; it doesn't do the checking. Rego will happily evaluate a lie.

Hashes are integrity, not authority. The attestation set hash proves the facts haven't been edited since. It says nothing about who produced them. If the document has to survive a hostile reading, it needs signing and a trusted time source on top.

Deciding and enforcing are still separate jobs. OPA can say whether the release satisfies the policy. Something else has to stop the release and keep the decision record next to the evidence it evaluated.

A bad policy renders beautifully. Uniform format is not uniform rigour, and the document will show you a nut-free check that never looked for nuts with exactly the same confident green shield.

And the real cost: every control needs an attestation schema and a policy as code before it can produce any of this. That's the work. The format is the easy part.

## Where to start

Pick one control. Define the facts it consumes and where they come from. Write the rule down in Rego, badly if necessary — the first version only has to be honest, not complete. Make the evaluation emit its own workings, render the document, and show it to someone in audit.

Then refuse to accept a different shape for the next one.

Get that right and nobody has to argue about the output, because anyone can replay the decision and get the same answer. Audit the *system*, not the output.
